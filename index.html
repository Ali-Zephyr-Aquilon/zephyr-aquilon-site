AI is accelerating drug discovery and validation. A boom in new molecules and treatments is coming, to cure the likes of cancer, brain disorder, rare diseases and infectious diseases.

However, how we administer them has not moved in decades. Patients are given bulky, awkward devices and the last thing they want is to display their hardship. It’s time to upgrade the way we administer drugs by solving a major bottleneck.

Delivery can be discreet, safe and precise.

The problem

The lung is the obvious route. It has stayed closed anyway.

Biologics are the fastest-growing class of medicine and almost all are injected. Injectors are simply inconvenient: of 125,474 US adults newly prescribed a GLP-1, about half had discontinued within a year (Rodriguez 2025).

Why not a pill? Because only a fraction of orally administered large molecules makes it through the metabolism.

The lung is the obvious alternative leveraging a vascularized surface of 70–100 m², about half a tennis court. Existing technologies such as Dry Powder Inhalers and Nebulizers fail to deliver a satisfactory aerosol. But it's not a verdict on inhalers.

We studied them to create market-and-product brief.

The platform

Faraday Wave Atomization

The MEMS Nebulizer amplifies small piezo vibrations step by step, so the liquid at the tip vibrates and pinches off. This phenomenon is known as Faraday wave atomization.

With acknowledgement to the originators. invented by distinguished Professors Chen S. Tsai and Shirley C. Tsai at UC Irvine, who invented the concept explicitly for inhalation (2010, 2009, 2017). We build from that foundation.

Why particle size matters

Only one narrow band reaches the deep lung.

A tight droplet distribution is necessary to ensure:
1) Maximized bioavailability,
2) Increased dosing precision,
3) Minimum throat impaction and therefore irritation
4) Reduced drug waste

Why low-shear matters

Lipid nanoparticles are the sharpest case.

LNPs made RNA medicines possible. However the capsule does not survive conventional nebulization — published work reports particle growth, aggregation and cargo loss from shear and interfacial stress (Wiebe 2026, Heiser 2025). Integrity largely holds below roughly 5–10 J/g of energy dissipation threshold (van Rijn 2023).

The urgent problem we are solving

Respiratory diseases

Asthma, COPD, cystic fibrosis. COPD alone affects more than 400 million people and is the third leading cause of death worldwide (Lancet Respir. Med., 2025), with cases set to rise 23% by 2050 (WHO/ERS). Those drugs are already approved for inhalation. Dose consistency and form factor are enough to compete there.

Beyond respiratory

Systemic biologics

If a fragile molecule can be aerosolized intact, this becomes a new capability for pharma. New possibilities create new markets: the whole-biologics universe is sized at $400–600B today and is set to exceed $1 trillion this decade.

Competition

A crowded field stuck on poor technologies.

We benchmark against vibrating mesh because it is the standard way to turn a liquid into a breathable aerosol. Mesh devices from Pari, Aerogen, Omron and Philips are good products, approved, at scale, refined over two decades. They are pushing liquid through holes and making the holes better. Beside being bulky, the Vibrating Mesh Nebulizer shears proteins hard enough to unfold and aggregate them, and a damaged dose provokes an immune response rather than merely underperforming (Lundahl 2021).

Dry powder is the other serious platform: expedient for pharma, but the complexity lands on users. Afrezza, a dry-powder insulin inhaler, was approved and proved a systemic biologic could be delivered by the lung; yet it never translated into market success: bulky, unable to ensure precise dosing, and burdened by weekly cleaning and mandatory lung-function testing that confused prescribers and patients alike. The pharmacology worked; the product failed.
